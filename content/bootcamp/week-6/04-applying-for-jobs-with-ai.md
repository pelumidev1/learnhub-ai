AI has made it easy to send three hundred applications in an afternoon. So everyone does, and recruiters now read piles of near-identical AI-written cover letters. The way to stand out is the opposite: fewer applications, each one clearly written for that company, each one with proof attached.

## Three good applications beat three hundred

A generic application sent to everyone is almost never read closely. A specific one, sent to the right company, often is. This course asks for three, and it means three done properly.

Choose three roles that match your path from lesson one. For each, you should be able to say in one sentence why you, specifically, would be good at *this* job.

## Research the company

Before writing a word, spend twenty minutes on the company: their website, their last few posts, the job advert, and anything recent about them.

Paste it into your Claude Project and ask:

> *"Here is the job advert and what I found about the company. What problem is this role really being hired to solve? Which one of my projects in context/projects.md is closest to that problem? What should I ask them in an interview?"*

Chidi finds that a logistics firm is hiring an "operations associate" to cut down manual reporting. His bank-statement automation is almost exactly that problem. That's the centre of his application.

## Tailor, don't rewrite

Keep one strong base CV from lesson three. For each application, change only what matters:

- **The order of your projects:** the closest one first.
- **The words:** use the advert's own words where they're true of you.
- **A short cover note:** three short paragraphs at most.

Draft the cover note with Claude, then make it yours:

> *"Draft a cover note for this role, under 150 words. Open with the problem the role solves and the project of mine closest to it, with its link. Use my voice from context/voice.md. No phrases like 'I am writing to express my interest'. Nothing that is not in my files."*

Read it aloud. If it could have been sent to any company, rewrite the first line until it could only be sent to this one.

## Show, in the application

Every application gets at least one link to something you built, placed where it will be seen: the first lines of the cover note, and the top of the CV.

If you can, go one step further: make something small for *them*. A two-minute screen recording showing how you would automate one of their visible tasks, or a quick improvement to something on their website. One application like that does more than fifty generic ones, because it shows them you can do the job before they've asked.

## Follow up, and spot the scams

**Follow up once,** about a week later, politely and briefly: you applied, you're still interested, here is the link again. Then move on.

**Spot the scams.** Fake job offers target people who are looking for work, and AI makes them look more convincing. Walk away if:

- they ask you to pay for training, equipment, a "registration fee" or anything else before you start
- the offer arrives without any real interview
- they want your bank details, BVN or ID before a signed contract from a company you have checked exists
- the pay is far above normal for the work described

A real employer pays you. They never ask you to pay them to get the job.

## What you should be able to do after this

Choose three roles worth applying for, research each company, tailor your CV and write a cover note that could only go to that company, lead with proof, follow up once, and recognise a job scam.
