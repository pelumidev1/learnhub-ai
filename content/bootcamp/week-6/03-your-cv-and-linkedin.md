Most CVs say what someone is. Yours can show what you have done. "Skilled in AI tools" is a claim anyone can type. A link to a live website you built, with a chatbot on it and an automation behind it, is proof. People hiring for AI work increasingly ask one question first: *"Can I see something you made?"* You can now say yes.

## Proof beats claims

Every line on your CV and LinkedIn page should pass one test: **could someone check it?**

- *"Proficient in automation"* cannot be checked.
- *"Built an n8n workflow that turns daily orders into a delivery plan, running every morning since October"*, with a screenshot, can.

Numbers help only when they are true and you could defend them. "Saves about three hours a week" is fine if it does. Never round up, never invent. An interviewer who catches one inflated number stops believing the rest.

## Your projects are your experience

If you have little or no work history in this field, your six weekly projects and your final project **are** your experience. Put them in a **Projects** section near the top, above older, unrelated jobs.

Each project gets three lines:

1. **What it is,** in one sentence, with the link.
2. **What you did,** with the tools named. Employers search for tool names.
3. **What it achieves,** honestly.

> **Order and delivery system for a Lagos bakery** · [link]
> Built a website with online ordering and a sign-in area using Claude Code, Supabase and Vercel; added an AI chatbot and an n8n automation that sends a delivery plan every morning.
> Replaced order-taking by WhatsApp message; used daily by the business owner.

## Rebuilding your CV with AI

Your context folder already holds most of what a CV needs. Use it, and use the job adverts from last lesson:

> *"Using my context folder and my projects in context/projects.md, draft a one-page CV for [the path from lesson one]. Lead with a Projects section. Use the words that appear in these job adverts where they are true of me. Do not add any skill, number or result that is not in my files. Mark anything you are unsure of with [CHECK]."*

Then edit it yourself, line by line:

- Keep it to **one page.**
- Keep the layout plain: simple headings, no tables or columns, no photos. Many companies read CVs with software first, and fancy layouts confuse it.
- Read every line aloud and ask: *is this true, and could I talk about it for two minutes in an interview?* If not, cut it.
- Run your voice skill from week two over it, so it does not read like every other AI-written CV.

## Your LinkedIn page

Recruiters search LinkedIn by keywords. Your page should say one clear thing: the path from lesson one.

- **Headline.** Not "Aspiring professional". Say what you do and for whom: *"I build AI automations for finance and operations teams · n8n, Claude, Supabase"*.
- **About.** Four or five short lines in your own voice: what you do, who for, one project with its link, and how to reach you.
- **Featured.** Pin your best project links here: your live site, a video from week four, a post about a build. This is the first thing people see.
- **Experience and Projects.** The same three-line project entries as your CV.

## Posting about your work

The fastest way to be found is to show your work in public. One post a week about something you built:

- **What you built,** with a screenshot or short screen recording.
- **One thing that went wrong and how you fixed it.** This is what makes a post believable.
- **What you learned,** in one line.

Show the screen, not just the result. People trust someone who shows how a thing was made far more than someone who only shows the finished picture. And the question you want a stranger to ask after reading is: *"How did you build that?"*

## What you should be able to do after this

Rewrite your CV and LinkedIn page around checkable proof: your projects first, tools named, nothing invented, one clear direction, and a habit of posting your work in public.
