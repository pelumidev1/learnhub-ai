This lesson is about prompt chaining, and it's simpler than it sounds. Instead of one conversation that does everything, you run a sequence where each step does one job and hands its output to the next.

Think of it as running a team of six, one at a time, instead of talking to one assistant.

## The six

**Researcher.** Gathers the raw material. What's already out there, what people are saying, what the numbers are. It doesn't give opinions or write anything yet.

**Strategist.** Takes the research and decides. What's the angle, who is this for, what are we not saying. This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why the writing later feels like it could be about anything.

**Storyteller.** Turns the decision into a shape. What's the order, where does the tension sit, what does the reader need to believe first.

**Writer.** Only now does anything get drafted. And by this point the draft is mostly assembly, because the thinking is done.

**Skeptic.** A fresh conversation. "Pretend you disagree with everything written here." This is the step that catches what you were too close to see.

**Editor.** Cuts repetition, tightens sentences, and removes the parts that were only there because you liked writing them.

## Why the order matters

Each role has one job and only what it needs. The Writer isn't also trying to remember the research. The Skeptic has no attachment to the draft because it wasn't there when it was written.

The answers improve because each step asks a smaller question.

## One chain, start to finish

Amaka wants a post that fills her Christmas pre-order list. Here is the whole chain, one step at a time, each output pasted into the next.

1. **Researcher.** *"What do people in Lagos worry about when ordering a cake for Christmas? Late delivery, price, taste? List the worries, no advice."* She gets a list: delivery during Christmas traffic, cakes arriving damaged, bakers who stop replying in December.
2. **Strategist.** *"Here are the worries. My edge is same-day Lekki delivery and I reply to every WhatsApp within an hour. Which one worry should this post answer, and who is it for?"* The answer: people who were let down by a baker last Christmas. Answer the "stops replying" worry.
3. **Storyteller.** *"Give me the order for a short post: what the reader believes at the start, what changes their mind, what they do at the end."* Start with last year's disappointment, then her one-hour reply promise, then the pre-order deadline.
4. **Writer.** *"Write it in that order. Under 80 words. Include: pre-orders close 15 December, ₦25,000 for an 8 inch."* Now there's a draft, and it says something specific.
5. **Skeptic.** New chat: *"Pretend you are a customer who has been burned by a baker before. What in this post would you not believe?"* It flags "we never let you down" as a claim anyone could make. She swaps it for a real number: 212 orders delivered this year.
6. **Editor.** *"Cut this by a third. Keep the deadline and the price."*

That's six short conversations, about half an hour in total. The alternative is an hour of arguing with one chat that still gives you "Order your Christmas cakes now!"

## Your turn

Take something you need to make this week and run all six steps. Keep every output along the way. You'll notice that four of these six steps are the same every time you do this. Those four belong in a skill, the way you built one in week one.

## What you should be able to do after this

Run a six step chain on a real piece of work, and feel the difference between arguing with one conversation for an hour and moving through six that each take five minutes.
