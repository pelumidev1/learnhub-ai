Most people using AI today started in November 2022 and think the story starts there. It doesn't, and knowing how we actually got here changes how you use these tools.

## The question came first

In 1950 Alan Turing asked whether a machine could hold a conversation well enough that you couldn't tell it from a person. He didn't ask whether machines could think, because nobody can test that. He asked something you could test.

This course works the same way. Nobody can tell you whether Claude understands you. You can check whether its output is good enough to use, and that's the question that matters here.

## It went quiet twice

The field promised too much in the 1970s and again in the late 1980s, and funding collapsed both times. People still call these the AI winters. Today's AI is far more capable, but the lesson still applies: judge a tool by what it actually does for you.

By the end of these six weeks, you'll have built enough with these tools to judge them yourself.

## 2017: the transformer

A paper called "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the transformer. Every model you use today, Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, descends from it. Five years later ChatGPT put a text box in front of it and a hundred million people arrived.

So the technology wasn't new in 2022. What was new was a simple way for anyone to use it.

## What the model is doing

A language model predicts what comes next. It was trained on an enormous amount of text and it learned the patterns in it, so given some words it produces the words that most plausibly follow.

That explains most of the strange things these tools do. It's why a model sometimes invents a citation: a source that looks real is exactly what tends to follow a sentence like that. Vague instructions get generic answers because the most ordinary answer is the safest guess. And the same question can get two different answers because the model is picking from likely words, not looking anything up.

Most important of all: **the model doesn't know what you're trying to do.** It only knows what you tell it, so most of the next six weeks is about telling it more of the right things.

## See it for yourself

Meet Amaka. She bakes cakes in Lagos and sells them on Instagram and WhatsApp. We'll follow her through this week.

She types: *"Write an Instagram caption for my cake business."*

She gets something like: *"Indulge in our delicious, freshly baked cakes! Perfect for every occasion. Order now!"*

It's generic, because the only thing the model knew about her was the word "cake".

Now she types: *"Write an Instagram caption for Amaka's Bakes. I deliver the same day anywhere in Lekki if you order before 2pm. This week's cake is red velvet, ₦25,000 for an 8 inch. My customers are mostly people planning last-minute birthdays. Keep it short and warm, no hashtags."*

This time the caption is about her business. The model didn't get any smarter between the two attempts. She just gave it more to work with.

Try it now with your own work: ask for something once with one line, then again with five lines of real detail, and compare.

The invented citation is just as easy to see. Ask for "three statistics about the Nigerian bakery market, with sources", then open every link. Some won't exist. The model isn't lying to you. A report title that sounds real is just what usually comes next in a sentence like that.
