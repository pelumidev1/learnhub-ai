# Pilot script: Week 1, Lesson 1

Narration is written to be read aloud at about 145 words a minute (~870 words,
~6 minutes). Each chapter matches a chapter in the lesson file, so its start
time becomes the lesson's chapter timestamp. `[VISUAL]` lines are direction for

Look throughout: paper and white grounds, ink text, royal blue `#1F33CC` as the

## 0. Cold open (≈20s)

[VISUAL] Black-to-paper fade. A blinking text cursor, centre screen. Words type
in: "Write an Instagram caption for my cake business."

Most people met AI in November 2022, typed something into a box, and assumed
the story started there.

It didn't. And once you see the real shape of it, you'll use these tools
differently.

[VISUAL] The cursor line slides up into a title card: "What AI actually is."
LearnHub mark, bottom right.

## 1. The Turing test, 1950 (≈50s)

[VISUAL] A 1950 date stamp. A simple line illustration of two closed doors, a
person typing to each.

In 1950, Alan Turing asked a question. Could a machine hold a conversation well
enough that you couldn't tell it apart from a person?

Notice what he didn't ask. He didn't ask whether machines could think. That
question has no test. He swapped it for one you could actually check.

[VISUAL] "Can it think?" crossed out softly. Beneath it, in blue: "Is the
output good enough to use?"

That swap is the foundation of everything in this course. The question isn't
whether Claude understands you. The question is whether what it gives you is
good enough to use.

## 2. Why it went quiet twice (≈45s)

[VISUAL] A timeline runs left to right. A line rises in the 1960s, crashes in
the 1970s, rises again, crashes in the late 1980s. Each trough labelled "AI
winter".

The field then promised too much. Twice. Once in the 1970s and again in the
late 1980s, and both times the money left. People still call those years the
AI winters.

The pattern is worth knowing, because it repeats. The technology gets ahead of
what it can really deliver, everyone notices at the same moment, and the
excitement collapses.

[VISUAL] The timeline continues to today; a hype curve rises steeply. A thin
dotted line marks "what it can actually do" below it. The gap between them is
shaded.

Some of what you hear about AI right now lives in that gap. Some of it doesn't.
Six weeks from now, you'll be able to tell the difference yourself, because
you'll have built with it.

## 3. What changed in 2017 (≈50s)

[VISUAL] Timeline zooms to 2017. A paper's title page appears: "Attention Is
All You Need".

In 2017, a research paper called "Attention Is All You Need" introduced
something called the transformer.

[VISUAL] A single node labelled "Transformer" branches into three: Claude,
ChatGPT, Gemini.

Every major model you use today, Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, descends from it.

[VISUAL] Jump to 2022. A plain text box appears; a counter runs up to 100
million people.

Five years later, ChatGPT put a simple text box in front of that technology,
and a hundred million people arrived.

[VISUAL] A doorway opens onto the text box.

So the models weren't suddenly invented in 2022. They got a door.

## 4. What a model is actually doing (≈80s)

[VISUAL] The sentence "The cake was" sits on screen. Three candidate next words
fan out beside it with bars for likelihood: "delicious" (long), "ready"
(medium), "purple" (short). "delicious" locks in.

Here's the whole idea in one line. A language model predicts what comes next.

It was trained on an enormous amount of text and learned the patterns in it.
Give it some words, and it produces the words most likely to follow.

That one fact explains almost every strange thing these tools do.

[VISUAL] Three cards appear one at a time, each with a one-line label.

It explains why it sometimes invents a source. A real-looking citation is
exactly what tends to follow a sentence like that.

It explains why a vague request gets a generic answer. The most ordinary answer
is the safest prediction.

And it explains why the same question can get two different answers.

[VISUAL] The cards clear. One line, large, in Instrument Serif: "The model has
no idea what you're trying to do." A second line fades in beneath, in blue:
"It only has what you gave it."

And here's the idea this whole bootcamp is built on. The model has no idea what
you're trying to do. It only has what you gave it.

Everything in the next six weeks is about giving it more of the right thing.

## 5. See it for yourself (≈90s)

[VISUAL] A simple phone mock-up. Instagram-style profile: "Amaka's Bakes,
Lagos". A red velvet cake.

Meet Amaka. She bakes cakes in Lagos and sells them on Instagram and WhatsApp.

[VISUAL] Prompt types into a chat box, Geist Mono: "Write an Instagram caption
for my cake business."

She types: "Write an Instagram caption for my cake business."

[VISUAL] Reply appears: "Indulge in our delicious, freshly baked cakes!
Perfect for every occasion. Order now!" It greys out slightly.

And she gets this. Nothing's wrong with it. Nobody will remember it either. It's
the most average caption possible, because the model knew one thing about her:
the word "cake".

[VISUAL] A second, longer prompt types in. Key details highlight in blue as
they're read: "same day anywhere in Lekki", "before 2pm", "red velvet,
₦25,000 for an 8 inch", "last-minute birthdays", "short and warm, no
hashtags".

Now she tries again. Her business name. Same-day delivery anywhere in Lekki if
you order before 2pm. This week's cake is red velvet, twenty-five thousand
naira for an eight inch. Her customers are mostly people planning last-minute
birthdays. Short and warm, no hashtags.

[VISUAL] The new caption appears beside the old one. Side by side.

This time, the caption is about her, not about cakes.

The model didn't get smarter between those two attempts. It got more to work
with.

[VISUAL] A search-style list of three "sources"; two links break with a soft
grey "page not found".

You can see the invented sources just as easily. Ask for three statistics about
the Nigerian bakery market, with sources, then open every link. Some won't
exist. That isn't lying. A report title that sounds real is simply what usually
comes next.

## 6. What this means for you (≈35s)

[VISUAL] Clean paper ground. Two short lines appear as a checklist.

After this lesson, you should be able to do two things.

Explain to a friend, without jargon, what a language model is doing when it
answers you.

And predict which tasks it will be bad at, before you waste an afternoon on
one.

[VISUAL] A prompt box with a blinking cursor, empty.

Try it now with your own work. Ask for something once, in one line. Then ask
again with five lines of real detail. Compare the two.

That's the difference this course is about. See you in the next lesson.
