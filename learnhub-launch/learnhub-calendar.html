So, for us

The decision on 29 September was to follow the brochure and sell employability, which means competing head-on with every school in this table rather than around them. That is a harder fight and the copy has to be picked accordingly, because two of the arguments available to us are ones we lose.

We lose on proof. Darey has 145 Trustpilot reviews, a readiness score and a government logo. AltSchool has UK accreditation. We have no graduates. So no post below claims outcomes, quotes a student, or implies anyone has finished anything. The certificate is described by what it takes to earn, not by what it is worth.

We lose on scale. ALX has 123,000 followers and runs free. We are not going to out-post or undercut that.

What is left is real and worth saying plainly. Six weeks against their eight to fifty-two. Thirty-five seats against their two hundred. A live session every Saturday with the person who built the school. Seven things on the internet you can send to anyone. One paid tool, named, at $20. And a price printed on the page, which not one competitor in this table does. Darey redirects you to a login to find theirs.

SureSkill is the warning: 301 posts, 20 followers. That is what happens when a page posts about itself every day. So two of the fourteen days teach something with no ask attached, and they are the ones most likely to travel.

Nobody in this set writes text-led carousels that read like a short argument. They make posters, reels and application prompts. One sentence a frame, in the site's own type, is an open lane, and it costs nothing but writing.

A good prompt has five parts

Week two of the curriculum, given away whole. No ask until the last frame. One of the two posts most likely to be saved and sent on, so it gets the most design attention in week one.

Teach it freeOwner: Pelumi
LinkedIncompany page · 08:00

"Act as a world class marketer" is not a prompt. It is a wish. People treat prompting as a list of magic words. It is closer to briefing a contractor. What comes back tracks the quality of what went in, and what goes in has five parts. Context. What the situation is, who the output is for, what already exists. The model cannot see your business. Role. Not "expert", which means nothing. Something specific: an editor who hates adverbs, a customer who is about to leave. Constraints. Length, format, what to leave out. This is where most of the quality comes from, and it is the part people skip. An example. One sample of what good looks like beats two paragraphs describing it. Iteration. The first answer is a draft. Push back the way you would with a junior colleague. And when a conversation has too many wrong turns in it, start a new one with a better brief instead of correcting it for the tenth time. That is the whole skill. There are no secret phrases, and anyone selling you a list of them is selling you a list. Week one of the bootcamp builds your first skill, which is how you stop writing the brief again every time. Week two goes past all of this into workflows and the six roles, which is where one-off prompting stops working. Cohort one starts 12 October. learnhub.dev/enrol

Mon 5 OctD8

Seven signs a machine wrote it

The post most likely to be sent to somebody else, because everybody has received writing like this. It also proves the editing standard rather than claiming it.

Teach it freeOwner: Pelumi
LinkedIncompany page · 08:00

Writing with AI is easy. Writing with AI so that it still sounds like you is the actual skill, and almost nobody teaches it. You can tell in a sentence when a post came straight out of a model. The pet words: delve, leverage, seamless, landscape, tapestry, "in today's fast-paced world". The fake contrast, where it is never just a cake, it is a memory. Everything grouped in threes. The opener that announces itself before saying anything: "let's dive in", "here's the thing". Every paragraph landing on a tidy one-line moral. An em dash in every sentence. And the praise nobody asked for, "great question", "it's important to note that". None of that is the model's fault. It is what happens when people stop at the first draft and call it finished. None of those are hard to remove once you can name them. The one move that finds them for you: open a fresh chat, paste the draft, and ask it to mark every line that sounds like AI wrote it and say why, without rewriting anything. Then fix those lines yourself, in your own words. Week two of the LearnHub AI Bootcamp teaches the model your voice as a reusable skill, so the draft starts closer to how you actually write, with these tells on the skill's never list. Voice

Voice rules

The company page speaks, to one reader at a time. Pelumi's own accounts carry the first person and the history. These two voices do not mix, and the rule was set on 22 August after the masterclass page was written twice in the wrong one.

Always

  • Open on the reader's situation, never on the company name.
  • One idea per post. One call to action, one destination, and the destination is learnhub.dev/enrol.
  • Use the vocabulary above. Bootcamp, not programme. LearnHub, camel case.
  • Name what the student will have built, not what they will have learned about.
  • Say the price, and say the two costs that are not included.
  • Keep sentences short. Nigerian English rhythm is correct here and is not something to edit out.
  • Every figure in the mono face. It is the site's signature; keep it on social.
  • Read it aloud before scheduling. If a sentence sounds like a school addressing an intake, replace it.
  • Emoji in the three bios only, one per line. Never inside a post.

Never

  • "I". The company page is not a person. The story belongs on Pelumi's own accounts and gets reposted from there.
  • The 2025 closure, the cloud programme, the scholarships, or the student who paid ₦150,000. That is his marketing, not the company's.
  • Promise a job, imply a placement, or name a salary. The brochure's line is the ceiling: no bootcamp can promise you a job.
  • A testimonial, a student count, a completion rate or an outcome. Nobody has finished a cohort yet.
  • "Phone first" or "no laptop needed". A laptop and Claude Pro are both required and both are said out loud.
  • Scarcity language on top of a real cap or a real deadline. Thirty-five seats and midnight on the 10th are both true. "Filling fast" is not checkable.
  • Em dashes. Sentence case in headings. One exclamation mark per thousand words at most.
  • Stock photography of people in headsets pointing at screens.
